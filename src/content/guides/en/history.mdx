The Zanzibar paper describes its own ancestry in [§5 Related Work](../../#s5). It cites Multics, UNIX, POSIX ACLs, VMS and Taos for the data model; RBAC for roles; the IAM systems of Amazon, Google and Microsoft for the idea of one ACL store behind an RPC API; Facebook's TAO as a system of similar scale; and Lamport clocks, Spanner, Chubby and ZooKeeper for the consistency question. This page follows the same threads in time order.

## How the ideas lead to Zanzibar

The first multi-user systems had to decide who could touch what. In 1966 Dennis and Van Horn gave each computation a list of *capabilities*, unforgeable tokens that name an object and grant rights to it ([Dennis and Van Horn](https://doi.org/10.1145/365230.365252)). Lampson's 1971 access matrix showed that capabilities and access control lists are two ways of storing the same table, by row or by column ([Lampson](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/classes/fa01/cse221/papers/lampson-protection-osr74.pdf)). Multics took the column view: every segment and directory carried an ACL ([Saltzer](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/361011.361067)). Zanzibar is on the ACL side too: it answers "who can touch this object?".

Operating systems then made the lists cheaper and coarser, and later richer again. The paper traces this itself: in the first edition of UNIX, file flags say "whether owner and non-owner can read or write the file"; by the fourth edition there are read, write and execute bits "for owner, group, and others"; POSIX ACLs add "an arbitrary list of users and groups"; VMS "supports ACL inheritance for files created within a directory tree" (§5). Those four words, permissions, users, groups and inheritance, are what Zanzibar's data model sets out to support.

From the 1970s, models moved away from per-object lists. Bell–LaPadula and Biba let the system, not the owner, decide by labels; Zanzibar is discretionary, so they are contrast rather than ancestry. Role-based access control (1992) put roles between users and permissions; the paper says roles "are similar to Zanzibar relations" (§5). Attribute-based control, defined in NIST's 2014 guide SP 800-162, computes decisions from attributes at request time. Relationship-based control, named by Gates in 2007 and formalised by Fong in 2011, decides from relationships such as "member of" or "parent folder of", which compose ([Fong](https://pages.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/~pwlfong/Pub/codaspy2011.pdf)). The paper never uses the word ReBAC, but its relation tuples are such a graph.

What was new at Google was scale and consistency. The paper says Zanzibar "has been in production use for more than 5 years" (§4) as of the July 2019 paper, so it was running by 2014 at the latest; no public source gives a start year. It serves Calendar, Cloud, Drive, Maps, Photos and YouTube (§1). TAO, which the paper compares it with, offers "eventual global consistency", while Zanzibar provides "external consistency and snapshot reads with bounded staleness" so that it protects against the "new enemy" problem (§5). Lamport clocks were ruled out because they "require explicit participation of all “processes”", and some of Zanzibar’s are "external clients or even human users". Chubby does not support "high volumes of data, efficient range reads, or reads at a client-specified snapshot with bounded staleness", and ZooKeeper offers neither external consistency across nodes nor bounded-staleness snapshot reads (§5). Spanner and its TrueTime clock supplied the guarantee ([Spanner, OSDI '12](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi12/osdi12-final-16.pdf)).

After the paper, others rebuilt the design in the open. Ory Keto was rewritten around it in 2021, and SpiceDB, OpenFGA, Permify and Topaz followed within about eighteen months. At the same time policy engines in the ABAC line, OPA and Cedar, matured, and the OpenID Foundation's AuthZEN working group, formed in 2023, standardised how an application asks an authorization engine for a decision: its Authorization API 1.0 became an OpenID Final Specification in January 2026. The details of each system are on the ecosystem pages.

## What Zanzibar took from where

The paper is careful about the difference between building on something and resembling it. It runs on Spanner and Leopard; it supports the features of earlier ACL systems; and it calls RBAC roles similar to its relations and the "blessings" of Abadi et al. similar to its tuples (§5).

Notice the split. The data model on the top half is a recombination of old ideas; the paper's own claim is the unification, modelling with tuples "allows us to unify the concepts of ACLs and groups" (§2.1), where Abadi et al. kept them separate (§5). The bottom half is what made it work at Google's scale: Spanner for "external consistency and snapshot reads with bounded staleness" (§5), and Leopard, "an indexing system used to optimize operations on large and deeply nested sets" (§3), for groups that pointer chasing cannot serve fast enough (§3.2.4).

<Callout kind="note" title="IAM on top">

The paper also notes that Google's Cloud IAM system "is built as a layer on top of Zanzibar’s ACL storage and evaluation system" (§5): a role-based product running on the relationship store. See [ACL, RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC](../access-control-models/) for how the models fit together.

</Callout>
